Reading: Daniel Botkin's Discordant Harmonies

Daniel Botkin’s book Discordant Harmonies, published in 1990, argues that the dominant conceptual framework used to describe nature—and the analogies it encourages—is out of date. The current framework, which interprets the natural world as a mechanical system, was developed in response to scientific discoveries in the 17th and 18th centuries but has not been adequately updated since.

Before that, the natural world was seen as an ordered, organic whole, made for the purposes of humans. Botkin quotes Cicero, who after pondering the question and concluding that “reason is the highest attribute of all”, says “[w]e may therefore well believe that the world and everything in it has been created for the gods and for mankind.”1 Plato held “that ‘nothing incomplete is beautiful’ and that nature must be ‘the perfect image of the whole of which all animals—both individuals and species—are parts.’”2 The main exception among classical philosophers was Lucretius, who held almost the opposite view: “the world of ours was not prepared for us by a god. Too much is wrong with it.”3 Nonetheless, the majority philosophical view of the Greeks and Romans survived in altered form into the Christian era.4

Then there was the Scientific Revolution. In the 17th and 18th centuries, astronomers and physicists discovered simple, “universal rules” that “[t]he Earth itself obeyed.”5 Along with a series of new scientific tools and mechanical inventions, these discoveries “led to a change in metaphors, but more profoundly to a change in explanation; from a belief in the Earth as an organism created by the Great Artist to a belief in the Earth as a magnificent machine invented by the Great Engineer.”6

Discordant Harmonies focuses on the restrictions that this view had on ecology. “Machine age” ecology, Botkin writes, “implies and predicts” either constant animal populations or “exacting periodic oscillations”.7 Botkin’s own work studying the moose of Isle Royale, an island and National Park in Lake Superior, is a case study in the use of this sort of interpretive framework and its limitations. The island had been relatively free of human influence (“indeed less directly disturbed by human influence than the African plains and savannas”) and when moose arrived there in the early 1900’s, the moose’s main predator, the timber wolf, was not present.8

Without a real threat of predation, the number of moose increased so dramatically that they severely reduced the supplies of their preferred foods, water lilies and yew. As predicted by some scientists at the time, the “number of moose dropped from more than 3,000 to fewer than 500” in the 1930’s.9 Then, they began to recover.

They recovered so well that they began to be in danger, again, of suffering from their success. “After the island was established as a national park,[…] the park personnel became concerned that a second major die-off might occur.”10 The decision was made by park personnel to introduce the moose’s primary predator, the timber wolf, in the hopes that downward pressure on the population would prevent the moose population from crashing out every few decades. “It is an old belief that a predator is necessary to maintain the balance of the population of its prey.”11 The wolves were introduced in the 1940’s and their population slowly grew into the 1960’s, when Botkin began his study of the park.

Botkin began studying the moose of Isle Royale under the regime of the Lotka-Volterra equations, which attempt to describe changes in population by mathematizing the relationship between a predator species and a prey species. The Lotka-Volterra equations and the broader ecological thinking they supported “[predict] that populations achieve a constant abundance or undergo exacting periodic oscillations and that the growth of the populations is stable”.12 Other possibilities—that environmental factors were more important, that populations were never really stable, etc.—were not so much forbidden as not even considered.

It was of little import that the equations did not have sound evidentiary footing and could not, even in the context of bacteria in a laboratory setting, be shown to be predictive.13 The theory had outrun the practitioners; “As Kenneth Watt wrote in 1962, ecologists had tended to believe that their science had lacked theory, while in fact it had ‘too much’ theory—in the sense that the theory had been utilized and was influential even though it was not carefully connected to observations.”14 This bad equilibrium persisted in part because “physicists and mathematicians had the highest status among scientists” and in part because the ecologists lacked the technical depth and mathematical skill to convincingly criticize it.15

It also persisted because it was the late 1960’s and the machines that did the calculation were still primitive and the programs they were meant to run “forced constancy on the population.”16 The first of these calculators was a 15-pound Friden Calculator which was slow and noisy and took up the only outlet on the island.17 Even when they upgraded in the second year to a 450 dollar pocket calculator, the theory remained the same. The model, which happened to be easy to calculate, remained too mathematical for ecologists to understand.

In this conceptual context, Botkin began his research on the island. He and his team discovered, first off, that the character of the individual wolves and moose appeared to have quite an effect on the population of each species on the island.18 Furthermore, the team’s hypothesis that there was some mineral nutrient on the island which was limiting the growth of the moose population proved true, at least in part, and they settled on sodium as the limiting factor.19

This is all to say that there was, in the end, no standard state of equilibrium on the island. The existence, to say nothing of the quantity, of both the moose and the wolves was contingent and, in both cases, the species had only a tentative connection to the island. The first time the wolves entered the land (in recent memory), it was by being shipped in from a zoo in a pack of six. That did not work. The second time they entered the land (in recent memory) they did so “naturally”, walking over a frozen Lake Superior just like the moose had.20 (In 2018, more wolves were airlifted to the island from the mainland to top up a struggling population of two wolves.21)

What, exactly, is conservation? The word we use to describe Edmund Burke and the word we use to describe men and women sitting in redwoods and canoeists in the Boundary Waters share most of their phonemes. I remember, one of my twenty or thirty memories total, a teacher of Latin at college, sitting at a wooden table, explaining to us the weight of the prefix con- and the intensity it applied to the word with which it was married.22 The example he used was this one: conservare unites con- with servare, which means to keep or protect, to provide a combined meaning that was close to these: to safeguard, to cherish, to preserve.23 He spoke to us, hoping we might understand how strong was the impulse to keep safe that was described by “to conserve”, “conservation”, “conservatism”.

In Anton Chekhov’s Uncle Vanya (first staged 1899), there is a doctor named Astrov who is fond of not eating meat, of drinking, of complaining, and, of forests. He laments the extensive use of the woodlands that surround the estate at which the play takes place (Chekhov called an earlier version of the play The Wood Demon24). In the third act, the beautiful wife of the old professor tells Astrov that yes, she does want to see his “sketches”. He eagerly agrees. Her purpose is to interrogate Astrov about a potential romantic interest; his purpose, though he is fascinated by and infatuated with her, is to tell her about fifty years of canopy loss.

The sketches Astrov brings with him are three maps of the same area, each separated by twenty-five years, showing the presence and absence of certain types of vegetation and animal life. She, Helena, grows bored as Astrov walks through the changes to the landscape:

Now look here. The picture of our district as it was fifty years ago. The dark- and light-green colors indicate forests; half of the entire surface is taken up by forests. Where there’s red cross-hatching on the green, it means that moose and wild goats lived there … I indicate both flora and fauna. On this lake lived swans, geese, ducks, and, as old folks say, there was a power of birds of all sorts, countless numbers of them: huge flocks flying over. […] Now let’s look lower. There’s how it was twenty-five years ago. Here only a third of the whole area is under forest. No more wild goats, but still some moose. The green and blue are more pale. And so on, and so forth. Let’s go to the third part: the map of the district in our time. There’s some green here and there, not solid but patchy; the moose, the swans, the wood grouse have disappeared … Of the former hamlets, farmsteads, hermitages, watermills—not a trace. In general the picture of a gradual and unquestionable degeneration, which will take some ten or fifteen more years to become complete. 25

Faced with this sort of acute change, it is difficult to read the situation in any way but the way that Astrov reads it: the world was basically fine and then people began to ask more of the landscape, cutting down trees for housebuilding and for fuel, and the landscape suffered for it, withering and thinning out. The goal of conservation in this case is simple: first, stop the “regular and slow decline” and then, as able, restore the landscape to the way it was before.

The problem of Isle Royale is a little more complicated, if only because we know more. Like Astrov, Botkin surveys the natural history of the Boundary Waters canoe area in nearby Minnesota:

Moose meander here as they do on Isle Royale, and people come every summer to canoe and hike and to discover wilderness. But what is the true character of this wilderness that they seek? The history of the vegetation holds some clues for us. That history has been reconstructed using all three kinds of evidence: written history; existing forests; and lake sediments. Pollen deposits from the Lake of the Clouds within the Boundary Waters Canoe Area indicate that the last glaciation was followed by a tundra period in which the ground was covered by low shrubs now characteristic of the far North, as well as reindeer moss and other lichens and lower plants.

The tundra was replaced by a forest of spruce, species that are now found in the boreal forest of the North, where they dominate many areas of Alaska and Ontario. About 9,200 years ago the spruce forest was replaced by a forest of jack pine and red pine, trees characteristic of warmer and drier areas. Paper birch and alder immigrated into this forest about 8,300 years ago; white pine arrived about 7,000 years ago, and then there was a return to spruce, jack pine, and white pine, suggesting a cooling of the climate. Thus every thousand years a substantial change occurred in the vegetation of the forest, reflecting in part changes in the climate and in part the arrival of species that had been driven south during the ice age and were slowly returning.26

On Isle Royale, the changes were faster; there was the appearance and disappearance of, the increase and decrease of, both moose and wolves. Like the Boundary Waters, Isle Royale sported a special designation (it was now a National Park) and it was therefore expected to be “natural”. Because the changes happened so quickly, the question of “Which nature?” had immediate practical salience:

Was it natural to have the moose dying in large numbers? If so, should this really be allowed to happen when the moose were one of the park’s main attractions? Indeed, one could ask whether the presence of the moose was natural at all, since they had not been on Isle Royale, as far as was known, prior to European settlement of North America.27

To actively maintain the wolf population on the island represents only one of the possible solutions. In his conclusion, Botkin enumerates three modes of wilderness preservation, which serve to illustrate the practical outcome of the unpleasant conclusion that there is no singular natural state we can go back to.

The first is the “no-action wilderness” where humans do not modify the landscape. This does not recreate pre-human or pre-European environments. It is, instead, a “baseline from which scientists can measure the effects of human actions elsewhere”.28 It may be pleasant to spend time in but that is not the goal.

The second possibility is to aim for a”preagricultural wilderness”. This hopes to recreate as best as possible the landscape as it appeared at some specific time, often the time just before large settlement massively changed the landscape. What visitors want is often to see the world as it might have existed before European settlement or agriculture. Those in charge of the preservation must pick a time to emulate since, as with the Boundary Waters Canoe Area, there are many valid interpretations of “preagricultural” or “before human intervention”. Unlike the first option, this requires changing the landscape, by introducing or removing species, burning, applying pesticides, etc.

The third, “the conservation area”, is dedicated to the preservation of a particular species or a particular natural community. These also require active management.

While the practical conclusion to Botkin’s thesis is clear enough, the conceptual conclusion—which would answer Botkin’s complaint about the bad analogies used to describe nature in the introduction—is less clear. “When we had less power,” he writes, “we could live with myths.”29 Now what? He tries a few different answers.

First, he places his argument in the context of earlier ecologists. His conclusion is consistent with that of Paul Sears’s writing that “nature is not to be conquered save on her own terms”: “I have tried simply to give a modern view of ‘her’ terms”.30 This view, he writes, reworks Aldo Leopold’s claim that “[c]onservation is a state of harmony between men and land.” The harmony is there, still, he says, but it is “discordant”. The conclusion gives us “a view in which we are a part of a living and changing system whose changes we can accept, use, and control, to make the earth a comfortable home, for each of us individually and for all of us collectively in our civilizations.”31

The clunkiness of the phrase “Discordant Harmonies” itself gives reason for pause. He continues. In his final effort to land the plane, he turns to a new mechanical analogy:

Could that most magnificent machine of the twentieth century, the airplane, serve as the proper model for the system of nature visible below? […] We talk about the spaceship Earth, but who is monitoring the dials and turning the knobs? No one; there are no dials to watch, only occasional alarms made by people peering out the window,[…] We need to instrument the cockpit of the biosphere and to let up the window shade so that we begin to observe nature as it is, not as we imagine it to be.32

Back when software developers wrote and read computer code, they talked of “code smells”, practices and patterns that are—while not necessarily wrong—a sign of some underlying weakness in a program or a sign that a more obvious solution was not considered. You might get the same impression after reading that we should replace the prevailing, misleading analogy about nature with one that is too awkward ever to be adopted.

The option that Botkin’s book seems not to consider is that analogy itself is suspect. It is not exactly the problem that we are using the wrong metaphor for nature, though Botkin makes a good case that this is probable; a stronger form of his thesis would be that we rely far too much on metaphors and analogies—hoping that the experience we have with simpler systems and simpler situations will map usefully to the complexities of nature. But there is no ex ante reason to believe that nature will furnish us with some phenomenon which can serve as its own instructive microcosm.

After Botkin’s book was published, when the study of the natural world was well-established but the world of software was new, software borrowed analogies like ecosystem and web. This illustrates the way in which analogies are most useful when some phenomenon is new or particularly poorly-understood. With them, we hope to transfer some hard-won intuition from one domain to another where we have less experience or understanding. It is hoped that some proven correspondence between the subject and the reference is evidence that they will correspond in other ways. Dangerous territory. Better to understand nature on “‘her’ terms”.
